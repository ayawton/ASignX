Course: Fingerspelling Foundations: Your First Six Letters
Description: |
  Learn to form and distinguish A, B, C, O, L and Y through guided observation, handshape comparisons and camera practice. Build clear habits first, then recall the letters in mixed order and practise short letter sequences. This introductory course focuses on individual fingerspelled letters. Use the linked demonstrations and seek feedback from a qualified ASL instructor as you continue learning.

Lesson: Start with a clear, comfortable hand
XP: 10
Content: |
  Your goal: prepare to practise six ASL fingerspelled letters with clear, comfortable movements.

  Fingerspelling represents written letters using handshapes. It is often used for names and other words in ASL. In this course you will learn A, B, C, O, L and Y.

  Choose your dominant hand: usually the hand you naturally prefer for tasks such as writing. Right-handed and left-handed learners can both fingerspell. Keep using the same hand throughout these introductory drills.

  Keep your shoulder relaxed and your wrist comfortable. Position your hand where a person in front of you can see it. The palm generally faces outward at a comfortable angle; avoid twisting your wrist to force it perfectly straight ahead.

  Use this routine in each lesson:
  1. Watch each linked sign demonstration and notice the finger positions.
  2. Copy the handshape slowly.
  3. Lower your hand, look away from the guide and make the letter from memory.
  4. Compare your attempt with the demonstration before starting the exercises.

  For camera exercises, keep your whole signing hand visible with light falling on it. If detection struggles, pause, check the demonstration and adjust your framing. An accepted detection is feedback for the supported letter; human feedback remains valuable for the rest of your signing.

  Start with clarity. Increase speed only as forming the letters becomes comfortable.

Lesson: A and B: closed fingers or upright fingers?
XP: 30
Signs: A, B
Content: |
  Your goal: form A and B and explain their most visible difference.

  Watch both linked demonstrations before practising.

  Letter A represents the alphabet character A. Curl your fingers into a fist and rest your thumb beside the fingers. Notice the thumb's position along the side of the hand.

  Letter B represents the alphabet character B. Extend your four fingers upward together. Follow the linked demonstration for a comfortable thumb position; a bent thumb toward the palm is common, and thumb position can vary in fluent signing.

  Compare the main feature: A has curled fingers; B has four upright fingers together.

  Practise A, relax your hand, then practise B. Next, look away and try B followed by A. Check each against the demonstration. Make three careful attempts at each letter before beginning the exercises.

  Self-check: are you changing the finger positions deliberately, or just moving your entire hand? Keep the hand comfortably placed while forming each shape.

Lesson: C and O: an open curve or a closed curve?
XP: 30
Signs: C, O, A
Content: |
  Your goal: distinguish the opening in C from the contact in O, while remembering A.

  First, try A from memory. Compare it with its linked demonstration, then move on to the two new letters.

  Letter C represents the alphabet character C. Curve your fingers and thumb to make an open, rounded shape. Leave a visible gap between the fingertips and thumb.

  Letter O represents the alphabet character O. Bring your curved fingertips toward the thumb so they meet, forming a rounded shape.

  Watch the demonstrations to check the hand's orientation. For this comparison, concentrate on one feature: C stays open; O closes the gap.

  Practise C followed by O slowly. Next, relax and form O followed by C. Make the difference visible without squeezing your hand tightly.

  Recall drill: look away and make A, O and C in that order. Return to the demonstrations to check your finger positions. Repeat the letter that required the most thought, then try the sequence again.

Lesson: L and Y: choose the finger that stays extended
XP: 30
Signs: L, Y, C
Content: |
  Your goal: form L and Y without mixing up the extended finger.

  Begin by forming C from memory, then compare it with the linked demonstration.

  Letter L represents the alphabet character L. Extend your index finger and thumb. Keep the other three fingers curled. Notice the L-like shape made by the index finger and thumb.

  Letter Y represents the alphabet character Y. Extend your thumb and little finger while keeping the index, middle and ring fingers curled.

  Both letters extend the thumb. The key difference is the other extended finger: the index finger for L, and the little finger for Y.

  Watch both demonstrations. Practise L and Y slowly, then lower your hand and recreate each from memory. Check that the three fingers meant to be curled are actually curled.

  Mixed recall: try Y, C and L. Compare each attempt with its demonstration. Give your weakest letter another careful attempt before starting the exercises.

Lesson: Mix, recall and connect your six letters
XP: 50
Signs: A, B, C, O, L, Y
Content: |
  Your goal: recall all six letters in an unfamiliar order and begin connecting them smoothly.

  Before looking at the demonstrations, try this order: Y, A, O, L, B, C. Notice which letters you can form confidently and which make you pause. Check the uncertain letters against their demonstrations.

  Ask a practice partner to point to the written letters in a different order, or write them on small cards and shuffle them. Form one letter for each prompt. Check your work after each attempt.

  Next, practise these short sequences using the letters you have learned:
  CAB
  BAY
  LAY
  COAL

  Begin slowly and keep your hand in a consistent, comfortable area as the fingers change position. Aim for distinct letters and smooth transitions. Practise the sequences separately from the exercises below, which check individual letters.

  Choose one sequence, look away from the guide and fingerspell it from memory. Then compare its individual letters with the demonstrations. Ask a partner to identify what you produced when possible.

  After the exercises, choose the two letters that felt least reliable. Practise each, then mix them with two easier letters. Return on another day and try the mixed order before replaying the demonstrations.

  Use your camera result as one piece of feedback. Continue practising with demonstrations and people who can give informed feedback on your fingerspelling.
